Why Do I Keep Doing Small Tasks Instead of Important Work?

Articles›Procrastination›Small tasks instead of important work

You sit down to finish a proposal for a client. It's almost ready: you just need to confirm the price, check the final page, and send it. But first, you answer an email. Then you fix a spreadsheet. Then you remember that you've been meaning to organize your files.

You're not doing nothing. Quite the opposite—you've been busy all day. Yet by late afternoon, you realize the most important task still isn't done. Tomorrow, you'll have to face it again.

Why does this happen? Not because the smaller tasks are more important or more interesting. Sometimes they simply let you avoid, for a while, the unpleasant feeling that comes up when you think about the main task. A small task isn't the cause of the delay. It becomes a convenient way out of internal resistance.

Resistance shows up before you can explain it

Pay attention to the moment just before you switch tasks. You open the proposal again. You picture yourself setting the final price and sending it. And something changes.

A second ago, you felt reasonably at ease. Now there's tension inside—a sense of internal resistance. It may come as heaviness, irritation, anxiety, or a barely noticeable urge to put off this particular step. Your mind immediately offers a respectable alternative: “I'll just check my email first.” And suddenly you're working on something else.

The tricky thing about this resistance is that it doesn't necessarily say, “I'm afraid” or “I don't want to.” Sometimes it shows itself only in where your attention goes: you look at the important task, and almost immediately start looking for a way away from it. The explanation comes afterward. And it's perfectly convincing: that email does need an answer, the spreadsheet could use some work, and the files won't organize themselves.

When you switch, the unpleasant feeling may ease. There's a brief sense of relief: you don't have to face the main decision right now. But the task is still there. The next time you return to it, the resistance comes back—along with frustration over the time you've lost.

Day after day, you may find yourself struggling not with the work itself, but with your reaction to it. The smaller tasks only postpone that struggle.

What's behind the unpleasant feeling?

Let's return to the proposal. You need to send the price to the client. The actual action takes only a few minutes. But for you, it may mark the moment when your work will be judged—and when you can no longer keep refining it indefinitely.

Suppose a thought appears: “The client will say no.” That's an anticipated outcome, not something that has happened. If that possible rejection feels like “I'm not good enough at my job,” you may recognize a personal Meaning in the situation. The anxiety, or another emotion you can directly feel, is what we call emotional Charge in the Shadrin Pattern Method.

But it's important not to invent an explanation after the fact. Not all resistance is about fear of rejection. Not everyone can identify a clear Meaning in words. Sometimes the emotional reaction is felt first, and no words come with it. You can work with an experience you actually feel without assigning it an imagined cause.

For this article, what matters is something else: while your attention is busy finding the next small task, your reaction to the important one goes unnoticed. You see the behavior—“I got distracted again”—but miss the moment when the urge to retreat appeared.

How to notice resistance before switching again

Next time your hand reaches for your inbox instead of the main document, try to catch that moment of transition. You don't need to interrogate yourself, much less force yourself to suddenly enjoy the work.

Look at the specific action you were about to take: “All I need to do is set the price and send the proposal.” Then ask yourself:

“What did I just feel when I came back to this step?”

Don't search for the right answer. You might notice tension in your face, a heaviness inside, anxiety, or simply a sudden urge to do something else. If you don't feel anything in particular, don't invent it. The point isn't to find Charge at all costs. It's to notice your actual reaction, whatever it is.

Then ask a second question:

“When I'm about to check my email, am I really choosing that task—or am I trying to get away from an unpleasant feeling as quickly as possible?”

This is the moment when you may have a chance to stop following the familiar pattern automatically. You can notice the impulse to switch and decide what to do next without treating that first impulse as an order.

If you can return to the action you chose, return to it: finalize the price, send the proposal. Don't turn self-observation into one more reason to postpone work until you feel completely ready. Sometimes you notice resistance and can still take the next step. If the same familiar reaction repeatedly takes over your choice, it's worth working with that reaction separately.

In the Shadrin Pattern Method, recognizing the reaction is the beginning of an investigation, not a ready-made cure for procrastination. If you can identify a directly felt emotional Charge, further work involves observing it and checking whether it actually changes. Observation is the tool; immediate relief alone doesn't prove a lasting result. The real test comes later, when you encounter a similar task and see how you respond.

The most useful shift at this stage is that you stop treating small tasks as the main enemy. Your attention returns to the moment when your intention to do important work meets internal resistance.

Getting back to what matters

You wanted to finish the proposal, not spend the day endlessly preparing to finish it. So the answer isn't necessarily a stricter to-do list or another round of self-criticism. What matters is recognizing what keeps pulling you away from the action you've already chosen.

When resistance becomes noticeable, you have a chance to examine it instead of automatically hiding behind the next useful little task. And the question changes. Instead of “Why did I spend another whole day doing everything except what mattered?” you can ask:

“What exactly happens inside me when I'm about to do the thing that matters?”

In my book Procrastination: Stop Fighting Yourself, I explore more deeply how internal resistance arises and how to work with emotional Charge through the Shadrin Pattern Method. The goal isn't to become an even stricter boss to yourself. It's to regain the ability to do the work you've chosen without spending so much energy fighting yourself.
