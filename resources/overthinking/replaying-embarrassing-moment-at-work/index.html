Why You Keep Replaying an Embarrassing Moment at Work

Articles›Overthinking›An awkward moment at work

The meeting is over. You're back at your desk, but one moment keeps coming back to you. Your manager asked a question. You gave the wrong answer. They corrected you in front of your colleagues, and the discussion moved on. It seems like an ordinary moment at work. Anyone can make a mistake. But hours later, you're still thinking about it. What did your manager think? How will they treat me now? Will they decide I'm not competent enough? Could this affect my position at the company? You replay the conversation, their expression, their tone, your colleagues' reactions. You're trying to understand what that moment might mean for you. But you don't have an answer. And it's that uncertainty that won't leave you alone.

When a mistake leaves uncertainty behind

The wrong answer has already been given. You can't take it back. But you don't know what the consequences will be. Maybe your manager didn't think much of the mistake. Maybe they drew conclusions. You simply don't know. And that uncertainty creates tension. You wonder whether their opinion of you has changed, whether it will affect their trust in you, or whether the mistake could limit future opportunities at work. You don't have enough information to answer those questions. So your mind returns to the details you already know. How did your manager look at you? What tone did they use when correcting you? Why did your colleague stay silent?

It's as if you're trying to extract information about the future from a conversation in the past. But a memory can't reliably tell you what will happen next. The uncertainty remains.

Why the same situation affects people differently

Imagine two employees making the same mistake in a meeting. The first acknowledges it, learns from it, and gets back to work. The second spends the entire evening replaying what happened, trying to work out how their manager's attitude might change and what to expect next. Neither knows for sure what the manager thought.

Yet for one person, the unknown isn't a serious problem. For the other, it becomes a source of persistent inner tension. Why? Perhaps the difference lies in the meaning each person attaches to the possible consequences.

For one, the mistake is simply part of working life. For the other, it may feel like a threat to their professional reputation, the respect of others, or their own sense of who they are. But even that isn't the final answer. Why does the mere possibility of losing respect or appearing incompetent provoke such a painful reaction?

It hurt. But why?

You can give a perfectly reasonable explanation: “I'm worried because I don't know how this will affect my job.” But notice your reaction itself.

Why does this particular uncertainty affect you so strongly?

Why does the possibility that your manager might see you differently create so much tension? Why is it so hard to leave the question unanswered until you have more information?

Perhaps it's not only about what happened in the meeting. Perhaps the situation touched something that already held special meaning for you. For one person, the possibility of losing respect is especially painful. For another, it's the prospect of not being good enough. For someone else, it's losing confidence in their own position.

These are only examples. They don't mean that any one of them must explain your reaction. But they offer another way to look at what happened. Instead of asking only, “What will happen now?”

Ask: “Why does the possibility of that outcome affect me so deeply?”

Behind the scenes of our character

We often explain our reactions as personality traits: “I worry too much about mistakes.” “I care what people think of me.” “I don't like uncertainty.” Over time, these explanations can harden into a fixed idea of ourselves: “That's just the way I am.”

But what if some of the traits we take for granted involve recurring, automatic reactions? We can spend years treating them as inseparable from our character without noticing what triggers them or the personal meaning beneath them.

This is what we might call what happens behind the scenes of our character.

On the surface, we see someone who is deeply affected by a mistake at work and can't stop thinking about its possible consequences. Behind that visible response may be a chain of internal processes: the meaning assigned to the event, the emotional experience, and the familiar attempt to resolve uncertainty by thinking it through. When all our attention stays on the conversation with the manager, those processes can go unnoticed.

How the Shadrin Pattern Method approaches this

The Shadrin Pattern Method distinguishes three parts of the experience.

Event. You answered your manager's question incorrectly.

Personal meaning. You interpreted the mistake as a possible threat to your position, reputation, or the respect of others.

Emotional reaction. Uncertainty about the consequences is accompanied by anxiety, shame, tension, or another painful feeling.

Uncertainty alone doesn't explain why one person can live with it while another continues to struggle. In the method, attention turns to the personal meaning of the situation and the emotional charge connected with it. Working with that charge is not only a way to understand your reaction, but an opportunity to work through it and reduce the hold of a familiar emotional response.

This doesn't replace dealing with real problems at work. If a mistake needs correcting, correct it. If you need to speak with your manager, that conversation may be necessary. But when the practical issue has been addressed and the emotional experience keeps returning, the reaction itself may be what needs attention.

From understanding to working through your reaction

You may keep replaying what you said in the meeting, how your manager responded, and what your colleagues might have thought. But consider something else.

Why did THIS particular moment affect you so deeply?

Perhaps it's not only about what happened in the meeting. Perhaps the situation touched something that already held special meaning for you. And then the question changes.

Instead of “Why can't I stop thinking about this?” you begin asking: “What EXACTLY was so painful for me about this situation?”

That opens a different path: not endlessly replaying what happened, but examining and working through your own reaction.
