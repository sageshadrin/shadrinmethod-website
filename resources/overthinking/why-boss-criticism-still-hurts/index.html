Why Your Boss's Criticism Still Hurts Hours Later

Articles›Overthinking›A comment that still hurts

The meeting is over. You've corrected the numbers in your report, sent the revised file, and moved on to your other tasks. But one thing your manager said is still pounding in your head:

“You didn't check these figures carefully enough.”

An ordinary comment at work. The error was found, fixed, and the report accepted. Yet hours later, as evening sets in, you're replaying that moment: the cold tone, the pause before the words, the looks on your coworkers' faces.

There's a heavy, gnawing tension inside you. You defend yourself in your head, remember the sleepless nights you put into the project, and keep telling yourself, “It's not a big deal.”

But you can't talk yourself out of it. It's not having to recalculate the figures that hurt. It's what those words mean to you personally.

Why ordinary criticism can feel like a blow

Your manager pointed out an error in a document. That's the fact. But in that same instant, your mind supplies a private interpretation:

  • A flash of shame: “He's disappointed in me. Now everyone in the room has seen how weak I am.”
  • A threat to your standing: “I've lost their respect. They've written me off as a professional.”
  • A sense of injustice: “They've dismissed ninety-nine percent of my excellent work because of one typo.”

Your manager may not have meant anything beyond the correction, and your colleagues may have forgotten the exchange a minute later. But for you, the situation instantly became a personal threat.

This distinction is central to the Shadrin Pattern Method: there is the Event itself, your Perception of it, and the Meaning you give to what happened.

When the remark means nothing more than “there's a bug or typo to fix,” it doesn't wound you. But once it carries the Meaning “I'm not good enough” or “they don't respect me anymore,” a deep defensive reaction kicks in.

The trap of defending yourself in your head

Rationally, you know one small mistake doesn't erase your competence. You know your manager makes mistakes too, and the project as a whole was successful.

But logic is powerless against an activated emotional charge.

That evening, the argument continues in your head:

“I handled that entire sprint on my own...”

“I should've shown him the chart on slide four right there...”

It feels as though you're analyzing what happened at work. But you're fooling yourself. Your mind is desperately searching for arguments to quiet the shame and restore your wounded pride.

You win the imaginary argument again and again, but you feel no relief. Logical arguments can't patch the hole left by an emotional reaction.

What actually needs your attention

When your manager's words come back to you, set aside the question of whether the criticism was fair for a moment. Shift your attention away from their tone and toward your own inner state.

Recall the exact phrase that made you tighten up, and ask yourself:

“What Meaning did I give those words? Who do I feel I am in this moment?”

Don't search for an academic explanation. Notice the physical response: a lump in your throat, heaviness in your chest, heat rising to your face, or a tight feeling in your upper abdomen.

That physical response can help you identify the emotional charge connected to the Meaning you gave your manager's words.

Positive affirmations won't solve this. Neither will telling yourself that “criticism is an opportunity to grow.” As long as that tension lives in your body, the memory keeps pulling you back to the incident.

How to separate your work from your sense of self

To get out of this obsessive loop, you need to distinguish two things:

  1. The professional fact: A mistake in the data needs fixing. A questionable demand from management needs a professional discussion. This is where practical action belongs.
  2. The inner reaction: Shame, fear of judgment, and all those imaginary defenses aren't facts about your competence. They're the emotional aftermath. This is where neutralization belongs.

When the emotional charge is neutralized, the memory stops feeling toxic. You still remember what your manager said, and you can still learn from it. But an ordinary correction no longer feels like a final verdict on your professional worth.

In my book Overthinking: Free Yourself From Mental Replay, part of the Shadrin Method series, I explain the Shadrin Pattern Method's step-by-step protocol for neutralizing the emotional charge that keeps us trapped in loops of shame and self-blame.

The goal isn't to grow a thick skin and stop caring. It's to hear feedback and stand up for your boundaries without burning yourself out through endless, humiliating arguments in your head.

A correction may require you to fix your report. It should never become a verdict on who you are.
