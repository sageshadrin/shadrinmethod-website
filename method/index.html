Why Do the Same Problems Keep Coming Back?

You notice that you're putting off important tasks again, spending too much time thinking things over, doubting your decisions, or reacting to certain situations in ways you wish you wouldn't. You know what you want to change, but you don't yet understand why the same thing keeps happening.

Perhaps you're just beginning to look for an answer. Or perhaps you've already tried a great deal: reading books, doing exercises, changing your habits, and learning to manage your thoughts and emotions. Some approaches helped. At times, you may even have felt that you'd finally overcome the problem. But as time passed, the familiar reaction returned.

It's like putting out a campfire while leaving a glowing ember beneath the ashes. You can barely see it, but under the right conditions, it can start another fire.

Something similar can happen with our problems. We try to change our behavior, overcome inner resistance, and cope with troubling thoughts. Sometimes we succeed. Then a familiar situation arises, an old emotional state returns, and we find ourselves back where we thought we'd already moved beyond.

Perhaps the problem is that we're trying to put out the flames when we need to reach the glowing ember.

This is the idea behind the Shadrin Method, an original, practical approach to self-help. It proposes working with the emotional charge that fuels our inner resistance and, like a glowing ember, can ignite another fire under certain circumstances.

The method aims to reduce the influence of recurring emotional reactions on our behavior, giving us greater freedom to act on our own decisions rather than familiar inner resistance.

What Is an Automatic Pattern?

Imagine a familiar situation. You need to do something important: start a difficult project, make a decision, speak with someone, or show other people the results of your work. You understand what needs to be done, have the necessary knowledge, and genuinely want to act. But at some point, resistance arises within you.

It might feel like anxiety, tension, uncertainty, irritation, or an urge to delay that you can't quite explain. Thoughts appear: “This isn't the right time,” “I need to prepare a little more,” or “What if it doesn't work?” You postpone the task, endlessly think through possible consequences, or abandon your original decision.

In another situation, it may look quite different. A single remark, a particular tone of voice, or an unexpected event may be enough to bring up hurt, fear, anger, or an immediate urge to defend yourself. The reaction comes so quickly and feels so familiar that you may only notice it after you've already begun to act.

Within the Shadrin Method, such recurring processes are described as automatic patterns of behavior, perception, and response. Automatic patterns are understood as habits and ways of behaving sustained by basic emotions: a particular situation triggers a familiar emotional reaction, followed by familiar actions. A person may understand perfectly well that a behavior isn't helping, yet understanding alone may not be enough to change it.

An important distinction: a pattern is not your personality or an unchangeable character trait. It is an acquired layer on top of your character, so to speak—a particular way of responding that shows up under certain circumstances. Someone may act confidently in one area of life while experiencing strong inner resistance in another.

Why Understanding the Problem Sometimes Isn't Enough

When we encounter an unwanted reaction, it's natural to try to change our thoughts or behavior. We tell ourselves not to be afraid, not to procrastinate, and not to worry about other people's opinions. We practice more positive thinking, make plans, develop discipline, and look for more effective ways to act.

All of this can be useful. Yet sometimes, despite new knowledge and skills, the familiar reaction keeps returning. We understand what's happening but still experience the same tension, fear, or resistance.

A Different Way to Look at Familiar Problems

The Shadrin Method begins with this contradiction. Why can someone clearly understand what they want, make reasonable decisions, and still return to behavior they themselves consider unwanted? Why do some changes come relatively easily while others require a constant struggle with oneself?

The method offers another way of looking at these difficulties. Rather than treating procrastination solely as a lack of discipline, persistent doubt as an inability to make decisions, or self-sabotage as a lack of willpower, it invites us to consider the recurring automatic patterns that may lie behind them.

This changes the question itself. Instead of endlessly asking, “How can I force myself to do the right thing?” we can ask, “Why do I keep reacting this way in certain circumstances—and what can I do about it?”

One Method, Different Problems in Life

Procrastination, overthinking, fear of failure, lack of confidence, and self-sabotage may appear to be entirely different problems. Yet similar patterns can sometimes be found beneath them: a person wants one thing, but a familiar internal reaction steers them back down the same path.

The Shadrin Method is an original approach to working independently with such recurring reactions. Its purpose is to offer a practical way to investigate and remove the inner obstacles that prevent people from acting in accordance with their own intentions.

This approach is the foundation of a series of books, each devoted to a specific problem. Readers don't need to study the entire method or read the author's other books first. Each book is a standalone practical guide that applies the approach to a particular difficulty in life.

Where Do You Start?

You don't have to try to change everything at once. Perhaps your biggest challenge right now is putting off important tasks. Or perhaps you're exhausted by endless thinking, second-guessing, or the feeling that you're standing in the way of your own success.

Start with the problem that matters most to you right now. Explore the book devoted to it, and discover how the Shadrin Method approaches working through that problem.

Choose your problem—and start there.
