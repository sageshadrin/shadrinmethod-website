About the Author · Sage Shadrin

The Invisible Glass Wall: My Story

Author and Creator of The Shadrin Pattern Method

On a warm July evening in 1981, I was staying with my grandfather in a village. I was eleven years old.

That evening, I was home alone, watching the Soviet television adaptation of The Hound of the Baskervilles.

It was already dark outside.

The tension on the screen kept building. A gloomy setting, unsettling music, ominous sounds. Then, suddenly, a monstrous hound with a glowing muzzle burst out of the darkness and lunged at a man.

I was frightened. Deeply. Genuinely.

So frightened that the fear did not end when the film did.

I had no idea then how profoundly that evening would change my life.

Fear settled inside me. And stayed there for decades.

The Wall No One Could See

Little by little, I began to change.

The easy spontaneity of childhood began to fade. I grew more withdrawn and took the initiative less often. Where I had once acted freely and without hesitation, I became overly cautious and unsure of myself.

People around me attributed these changes to growing up. Adolescence was beginning; my personality was changing. No one saw much cause for concern. They assumed it would all pass with time.

But it didn’t.

If anything, the problem became more pronounced as the years went by.

In certain situations, an invisible glass wall seemed to rise in front of me.

I knew exactly what I wanted. I knew what I needed to do. Sometimes I could picture, with perfect clarity, how I should act.

But between my intention and my actions stood a barrier I could not get past.

And the harder I tried, the more strongly I felt its presence.

I could reason with myself, make arguments, push myself to act. The wall remained.

Something inside me refused to obey my conscious decisions.

Back then, I knew nothing about behavioral patterns. I only knew that something was happening inside me that I could neither understand nor control.

Why couldn’t I act the way I myself believed I should? What, exactly, was stopping me?

Those questions marked the beginning of a search that would last for decades.

1991: The First Discovery

The late 1980s and early 1990s were a time of tremendous change.

Perestroika was transforming the country. New books were appearing, and knowledge and fields of study that had once been difficult to access were becoming available.

I remember how much people talked about the fall of the Berlin Wall. Old borders were coming down, and new possibilities were opening up.

My own invisible glass wall, however, was still standing.

I immersed myself in psychology and the study of the self. I read Dale Carnegie, explored different approaches to understanding human behavior, and later became interested in socionics.

Every new book gave me something to think about. Sometimes I felt I was finally getting close to an answer.

But the glass wall remained.

In 1991, I came across Self-Knowledge and Self-Cultivation of Character, a book by the Soviet psychologist Yuri Orlov, published in 1987.

One of the practices described in the book particularly caught my attention.

Before long, I had a chance to try it myself.

After a minor falling-out with some friends, I felt resentful. The reason was, all things considered, rather trivial. The resentment itself, though, was quite real.

I decided to experiment.

To my surprise, the emotional intensity of what I was feeling diminished noticeably.

The result intrigued me, but I did not yet understand its significance.

I simply thought I had found an interesting practice that might come in handy someday.

And I set it aside.

For more than twenty years.

2016: Another Clue

The years passed.

Work, marriage, raising my daughter, the ordinary demands of daily life—life went on.

My interest in understanding myself would fade for a while, then flare up again.

Sometimes I put the search aside for long stretches. But sooner or later, I always returned to the problem.

The glass wall had never gone away.

In 2016, I was listening to a lecture by a specialist who worked with fears and deeply held beliefs.

At one point, he said something that caught me off guard and made me think:

“Every fear has its own physical address in the body.”

I knew immediately what he meant.

My own fear had been with me since childhood, and I knew precisely where I felt it in my body.

For the first time in a long while, I had heard an explanation that spoke directly to my own experience.

I still did not know what to do with that insight.

Then I remembered my experiment from 1991.

Two discoveries, twenty-five years apart, were beginning to form a single picture. I could see a possible connection between them, and I decided to continue my search in that direction.

2017: The First Breakthrough

By then, I had spent decades searching.

I had read countless books, thought things through, and made attempt after attempt to understand myself.

But now I had a specific direction to pursue.

In 2017, when I was forty-seven, I deliberately tried an approach I would later call Neutralization for the first time.

And at last, I saw a tangible result in my work with the fear that had followed me for so long.

I was overjoyed.

After all those years of searching, I had finally achieved something concrete.

The moment mattered so much to me that I even made a note of it on my computer. Years later, that note helped me establish the exact date.

For a while, I believed the problem was behind me.

But after some time, the familiar reaction returned.

I was disappointed.

Even so, what had happened meant a great deal to me.

Now I knew firsthand: a reaction I had considered impossible to overcome for so many years could change. Even if only for a little while.

So I kept searching.

Only now, I was no longer searching blindly.

2022: The Fall of the Glass Wall

Over the next several years, I continued investigating the problem, trying to understand why the result I had achieved had been temporary.

In 2022, full-scale war broke out in Ukraine. My family—my wife and children—left the country, while I stayed behind alone.

Despite everything happening around me, I continued my research.

That summer, when I was fifty-two, another idea from the field of self-discovery helped me explore my old problem more deeply.

I now had not only decades of accumulated experience, but also a result of my own that gave me reason to keep searching.

I decided to try a different approach to working through the fear that had been with me for so long.

And this time, something I had been waiting for more than forty years finally happened.

The glass wall fell.

The fear that had settled inside me as a child disappeared.

But the most important part would take time to become clear.

Months passed, then years.

Situations that had once brought that glass wall up in front of me no longer triggered the old reaction.

The inner resistance that had kept me from acting freely for so many years was gone.

The fear did not return. And it still hasn’t.

To me, this meant that a problem that had accompanied me through most of my life had finally stopped determining my actions.

What I did not realize then was that the fall of that wall would mark not the end of my investigations, but the beginning of a new chapter.

After 2022: New Discoveries

The fall of the glass wall did not bring my search to an end.

On the contrary, that experience prompted me to look more closely at other aspects of my own behavior.

I gradually began noticing other familiar reactions I had never paid much attention to before.

I noticed how I sometimes sabotaged my own efforts, getting in the way of what I wanted to accomplish. At other times, I caught myself overthinking, returning again and again to things that had already happened. And I knew procrastination firsthand, too: I sometimes put off important tasks even when I understood perfectly well that they needed to be done.

Now I wanted to do more than understand these patterns. I wanted to try to work through them.

I began applying what I had learned to other patterns in my own behavior, observing the results, refining my ideas, and improving my approach.

It did not all come together at once. At times, I had to reconsider earlier assumptions, look for better explanations, and keep working.

But each experience helped me better understand the possibilities and limits of the approach I was developing.

I kept investigating and working through different aspects of my own behavior, building the experience that would later become one of the foundations of my books.

From Personal Experience to the Shadrin Pattern Method

Over the years, I had accumulated many observations and practical discoveries.

Gradually, I began to notice that problems which looked very different on the surface could have similar patterns beneath them.

What once seemed like separate, unrelated discoveries was beginning to form a coherent system.

The method did not emerge from a single flash of inspiration. It took shape gradually—through the books I read, the ideas of other authors, and, above all, my own experience.

I compared observations, tested results, refined my understanding, and continued investigating.

That is how my own approach took shape: The Shadrin Pattern Method.

Its purpose is to help people not only understand their automatic reactions, but also investigate and work through the behavioral patterns that may limit their freedom to make choices and take action.

I do not see my method as a universal answer to every human problem.

It is a practical framework rooted in decades of searching and personal experience—one I continue to develop and refine.

For a long time, this work was something I kept to myself.

The idea of sharing what I had learned with others came much later.

2025: An Unexpected Idea

For years, it had never even occurred to me that I might become a writer.

Then, in 2025, a small incident unexpectedly changed the direction of my thinking.

I was listening to part of a paid consultation by a blogger I followed.

The person speaking with him was describing difficulties in his life, and I immediately recognized familiar signs of behavioral patterns.

The consultant offered a few suggestions. I have to admit, they struck me as rather superficial, especially considering the price of the session.

But what surprised me most was the other person’s reaction. He was genuinely grateful and embraced the advice enthusiastically.

That made me wonder: if even that conversation had been helpful to him, perhaps my own observations and years of practical experience might help someone, too.

My first thought was to create a course.

But I am an introvert. Public speaking and constant interaction with large audiences have never been my strengths.

So the course remained just an idea.

Soon afterward, I came across an advertisement for a course on writing and self-publishing books.

I was intrigued, bought the course, and began learning something entirely new.

Gradually, I realized that books were exactly the format I had been looking for.

I could take the time to set out my observations clearly, share my personal experience, and offer readers practical tools for investigating and working through their own behavioral patterns.

That was how I began writing my first book.

2026: My First Book

In 2026, my first book was published: How Behavioral Patterns Hinder Us on the Path to Achieving Success.

For me, it marked the beginning of an entirely new chapter.

For the first time, the results of years of searching and personal investigation had taken the form of a book I could share with others.

But I soon realized I did not want to stop at one book.

Over the years, I had encountered different behavioral patterns in my own life. Each deserved its own closer, more thorough exploration.

Little by little, the idea emerged for a whole series of books devoted to specific problems in human behavior.

Today, that series is called Shadrin Method.

I wanted each book to stand on its own, so readers could choose the problem that mattered to them and find not only an explanation of what was happening, but also practical tools to investigate and work through it.

That is how my decades-long personal search became the work I continue to do today.

My glass wall finally collapsed in the summer of 2022.

And now I hope that what I have learned will help other people recognize their own behavioral patterns, investigate them, and begin working through them.

Sage Shadrin
